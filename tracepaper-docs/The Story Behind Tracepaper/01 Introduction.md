# The Story Behind Tracepaper

When Tracepaper was first conceived, it wasn’t born out of a desire to revolutionize modeling tools or dominate the software architecture landscape. It emerged from necessity—a solution to a problem I kept running into, again and again, in my own work. Like many good ideas, it started simple and unpolished but carried the seeds of something bigger.

## The Problem: Connecting the Dots

Software projects today are like ecosystems: intricate, interconnected, and always changing. I often found myself needing to document not just the *what* of a system, but the *why*. Why was a decision made? Why does this process exist the way it does? Why is this codebase structured like this?

Traditional tools—whether they were Word documents, static diagrams, or rigid wikis—failed me. They were either too static, too isolated, or simply too unwieldy to keep up with the natural flow of a project.

Then there was the challenge of **collaboration**. Decisions aren’t made in isolation, and documentation shouldn’t be either. I wanted a way to capture the entire lifecycle of a decision or a design—its inputs, its outputs, and most importantly, the discussions and iterations that led there.

## The Early Days: A JSON Model and Command-Line Tool

Tracepaper didn’t start as the sleek, modern tool it is today. The first version was rough. It was a command-line tool that worked with an unreadable JSON model. Honestly, it felt like a hack at times, but it was functional enough to prove the concept. It let me model processes, store relationships, and automate code generation.

It also exposed the biggest gap I hadn’t fully appreciated: **usability.** A tool’s power is irrelevant if it’s a nightmare to use. This realization became a turning point.

## Redefining the Modeling Experience

The turning point wasn’t a single moment but a series of incremental changes. My goal wasn’t just to make Tracepaper work; it was to make it *feel right.* I started refining the interface, rethinking how people interact with models, and making it possible for teams to collaborate naturally without friction.

The DSL behind Tracepaper evolved, too, from a patchwork of concepts to a cohesive language that could describe everything from commands to projections. By the time the first visual modeler was built, it was clear that this wasn’t just a tool for architects but for anyone designing systems—from developers to product managers.

## Staying True to Its Purpose

As Tracepaper grew, so did the temptation to make it all things for all people. I had to remind myself that Tracepaper wasn’t about competing with enterprise-scale solutions. It was about enabling **small teams** to work with clarity and confidence.

One of my guiding principles has been **minimizing the need for consultancy.** I don’t want Tracepaper to be a tool that requires a manual or hours of onboarding. It should work naturally for the people who use it, even if they’ve never seen it before.

## Building for the Future

Today, Tracepaper is much more than that scrappy command-line tool I started with. It’s a zero-touch deployment platform that supports trunk-based development. It’s a collaborative space for teams to design, refine, and build systems together. And yet, it still feels like we’re just scratching the surface.

The future of Tracepaper isn’t about more features—it’s about **better experiences.** My focus remains on making modeling seamless, intuitive, and deeply collaborative. Whether it’s empowering users to make decisions in line with their system’s spirit or enabling them to build robust integrations with minimal overhead, Tracepaper is about solving the problems I once struggled with—and helping others avoid them altogether.

## Why This Matters

If there’s one thing I’ve learned in this journey, it’s that tools don’t change the world—**people do.** But the right tools make it easier for people to focus on what really matters: building systems that work, grow, and evolve.
